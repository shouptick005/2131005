1. The Automobile, Its Systems and Mechatronics ★★★

1.1 The Automobile

Definition (lecture)
The automobile is a type of motor vehicle (two other types are the truck and the bus). It is a self-propelled vehicle that travels on land. It usually has four wheels, an engine provides the power to move it, and it carries people primarily for their personal transportation.

History in brief: the first automobile was a three-wheeled gas buggy built by Karl Benz in Germany (1885–86); Gottlieb Daimler built a four-wheel buggy the next year. In the USA the Duryea brothers built the first car (1893) and Henry Ford his first in 1896; Ford's cheap, mass-produced Model T (1908) "put America on wheels" — 15 million were sold in the next 20 years.

1.2 Major Components: Body and Chassis

An automobile is made of two main divisions, the body and the chassis (Figure 1.1).

ComponentFunction
BodyProvides the enclosures (compartments) for the engine, the passengers and the luggage or cargo; gives shape, safety and comfort.
ChassisThe operating assemblies mounted on the frame, i.e. everything needed to make the vehicle move and be controlled:
1. Engine (power plant)Produces the power to move the vehicle by burning fuel (chemical energy → heat → mechanical work).
2. Power train (drive train)Carries the power from the engine to the drive wheels: clutch, transmission (gearbox), propeller shaft, final drive and differential, axles.
3. Suspension systemAbsorbs the shocks of the tyres and wheels meeting bumps and holes in the road; supports the weight and keeps the tyres on the road.
4. Steering systemLets the driver control the direction of travel.
5. Braking systemLets the driver slow or stop the vehicle and hold it when parked.
6. Electrical systemProvides electricity for cranking the engine, charging the battery, and powering the lights and other electrical equipment (ignition, instruments, ECUs).

The lecture also lists the systems that support engine operation — the fuel system, ignition system, lubrication system and cooling system — and those that get power to the wheels — the power train, the clutch, manual transmissions/transaxles and automatic transmissions/transaxles. Each has its own chapter in this book.

1.4 Drive Layouts: FWD, RWD, 4WD and AWD

The way the engine is mounted usually tells which wheels are the drive wheels (Figure 1.2):

  • Front-wheel drive (FWD): engine mounted transversely at the front; power flows through a transaxle (gearbox + differential in one unit) to the front wheels. Compact, light, good traction under the engine weight.
  • Rear-wheel drive (RWD): engine mounted longitudinally; power flows through the transmission and driveshaft (propeller shaft) to the rear axle. Good weight balance, suits high power and towing.
  • Four-wheel drive (4WD) / all-wheel drive (AWD): some RWD vehicles also drive the front wheels. They have a transfer case that sends (transfers) some of the engine power to the front wheels as well.

How AWD works. Engine → transmission → transfer case, which splits the torque between a rear propeller shaft (to the rear differential) and a front propeller shaft (to the front differential); each differential splits its share between the left and right wheels. Because the front and rear axles travel slightly different distances in a turn, an AWD system has a centre differential or a multi-plate/viscous coupling in the transfer case that lets the front and rear shafts turn at different speeds — so it can stay engaged all the time, on any road. A part-time 4WD locks the front and rear shafts together and is meant only for loose surfaces (mud, sand, snow). Modern AWD is electronically controlled: wheel-speed sensors detect slip and an ECU-controlled clutch sends more torque to the axle with grip.

1.5 Mechatronics in the Automobile

Mechatronics is the synergistic integration of mechanical engineering, electronics, control and computer software in the design of products and processes. A modern car is a mechatronic system: its mechanical systems (engine, transmission, brakes, steering, suspension) are measured by sensors, controlled by electronic control units (ECUs) running software, and driven by electrically controlled actuators, in closed loops (Figure 1.3). A modern car has 50–100 ECUs linked by in-vehicle networks (CAN, LIN, FlexRay).

1.8 Past Question: Significance of Mechatronic Systems (2023)

2023 · Q1(b)Explain the significance of Mechatronic systems in advanced vehicle technology with an example.10Topic: automotive mechatronics

Mechatronics integrates mechanical systems, electronics (sensors, ECUs, actuators) and software control. Its significance in vehicles:

  1. Precision and adaptability: the ECU adjusts the system for every operating condition in real time (every engine cycle), which a fixed mechanical design cannot do.
  2. Fuel economy and emissions: exact air–fuel ratio and spark timing meet emission laws and reduce consumption.
  3. Active safety: ABS, TCS, ESP and airbags react in milliseconds — faster than any driver.
  4. Comfort and convenience: automatic transmissions, power steering, climate control, cruise control.
  5. Diagnostics and integration: on-board diagnosis stores fault codes; ECUs share data over CAN, so one sensor serves several systems.
  6. New functions: hybrid/electric drive, driver assistance and autonomous driving are only possible with mechatronics.

1.10 Past Question: All-Wheel Drive

2024 · Q2(a)What is all-wheel drive? Write down the advantages of using all-wheel drive. Also, describe how it works.11Topic: drive layouts; AWD

All-wheel drive (AWD) is a drive layout in which the engine power is delivered to all four wheels. In the lecture: some rear-wheel-drive vehicles also drive the front wheels; they have a transfer case that sends part of the engine power to the front wheels — this is four-wheel drive (4WD) or all-wheel drive (AWD). (Usually "AWD" means full-time, automatic, for all roads; "4WD" means a selectable system for off-road use.)

Advantages:

  1. Better traction — the driving force is shared by four tyres, so each needs less grip: better acceleration and hill-climbing on wet, icy, muddy or loose roads.
  2. Stability and control — less wheel spin and better cornering stability, especially in poor conditions.
  3. Safety — ECU-controlled AWD reacts to slip automatically, together with TCS/ESP.
  4. Towing and off-road ability (4WD with low-range gears).

Disadvantages (worth one line): extra weight, cost and friction losses → higher fuel consumption; more maintenance.

How it works (draw Figure 1.2, right): engine → transmission → transfer case, which splits the torque into a rear propeller shaft (→ rear differential → rear wheels) and a front propeller shaft (→ front differential → front wheels). The differentials allow the left and right wheels to turn at different speeds in a turn; a centre differential or a multi-plate (viscous or electronically controlled) clutch in the transfer case allows the front and rear axles to turn at different speeds and varies the torque split. When a wheel slips, wheel-speed sensors tell the ECU, which tightens the clutch (or brakes the slipping wheel) to send more torque to the wheels that have grip.

2. The IC Engine: Components, Valves, Valve Timing and Firing Order ★★★

2.1 The Internal Combustion Engine and Its Classification

The engine is the key part of an automobile: it produces the power that moves the vehicle. In an internal combustion (IC) engine the fuel burns inside the engine cylinder (petrol, diesel and gas engines); in an external combustion engine it burns outside (steam engine, steam turbine). Two kinds of piston engine are used in vehicles — spark-ignition (SI, petrol) and compression-ignition (CI, diesel) — which differ in (i) the fuel used, (ii) how the fuel gets into the cylinder, and (iii) how it is ignited. (The rotary Wankel engine is the only non-piston engine used in cars.)

2.3 The Four-Stroke Cycle and Engine Displacement

A four-stroke engine completes one working cycle in four piston strokes = two crankshaft revolutions (720°): (1) induction (suction) — piston moves down, intake valve open, mixture (or air in a diesel) enters; (2) compression — both valves closed, piston moves up; (3) power (expansion) — the mixture is ignited (spark, or injected fuel self-ignites in hot air), burning gas at up to 3316 °C and 4140 kPa drives the piston down; (4) exhaust (emission) — exhaust valve open, piston pushes out the burnt gas. Only one stroke in four produces power — hence the flywheel.

2.4 Pistons and Piston Rings

The piston (aluminium alloy, about 0.45 kg) is made slightly smaller than the cylinder so it can slide — a sliding fit. The small gap (piston clearance) must be closed; otherwise some compressed mixture and burnt gas leak past the piston into the crankcase. This leakage is called blowby; excessive blowby reduces engine power, wastes fuel and pollutes the air (and contaminates the oil). To prevent it, piston rings are fitted in grooves cut in the piston. The rings are split at one point so they can be expanded, slipped over the piston head and into the grooves; their own spring tension presses them against the cylinder wall.

  • Compression rings (top one or two grooves): form a sliding seal between the piston and the cylinder wall, holding the compression and combustion pressure and preventing blowby; they also conduct heat from the piston to the cylinder wall.
  • Oil (control) ring (lowest groove, slotted/expander type): scrapes off most of the lubricating oil splashed on the cylinder wall and returns it through holes in the piston to the crankcase, leaving only a thin film — prevents oil from entering the combustion chamber (oil burning, smoke, carbon).

2.5 Valves and Valve Trains

Purpose of valves. Each cylinder has at least two valves. The intake valve opens just before the intake stroke so that the air–fuel mixture (air in a diesel engine) can enter the cylinder; the exhaust valve opens just before the exhaust stroke so that the burnt gases can escape. With both closed, the cylinder is sealed for compression and power. Some engines have 3, 4, 5 or 6 valves per cylinder for better breathing.

Valve trains. Valves are opened by cams and closed by springs. Two basic valve trains: (1) overhead camshaft with bucket tappets or rocker arms, (2) camshaft in the block with pushrods (overhead valve, OHV). Arrangements: L-head, overhead-valve, overhead-camshaft, multivalve engines. The camshaft is driven from the crankshaft at half crankshaft speed by timing gears, timing chain or timing belt — called "timing" because they time the opening and closing of the valves. A tensioner (spring-loaded or hydraulic) keeps the chain or belt tight; a slack belt can "jump timing", causing bent valves, cracked pistons and severe damage.

Why the intake (suction) valve is larger than the exhaust valve. When the intake valve is open, the only force pushing the mixture into the cylinder is atmospheric pressure (≈ 101 kPa, minus the small depression created by the piston), so a large opening is needed to fill the cylinder well (high volumetric efficiency). The exhaust gases leave at high pressure (several hundred kPa when the exhaust valve opens) and are also pushed out by the piston, so a smaller exhaust valve is enough. A smaller exhaust valve is also easier to cool — it runs much hotter.

Materials and cooling. Intake valves: Cr-Ni-Mo steel. Exhaust valves run hot (up to 871 °C, red hot): Si-Cr steel or, better, austenitic steel (high Cr and Ni: better hot hardness, impact value, oxidation and corrosion resistance). The valve face loses heat to the seat each time it closes, the stem to the guide; coolant passages around seats and guides cool both. Some exhaust valves have hollow stems partly filled with sodium, which melts and carries heat from the head to the stem — they run about 93 °C cooler.

A valve timing diagram shows, in degrees of crankshaft rotation relative to TDC and BDC, when the valves open and close (and, for a diesel, when fuel injection starts and stops). In a real engine the valves do not open and close exactly at the dead centres:

  • Intake opens before TDC (lead) and closes after BDC (lag): the valve needs time to open fully, and the inertia of the moving air keeps filling the cylinder even after the piston passes BDC → more charge.
  • Exhaust opens before BDC: most of the pressure is released before the piston rises, reducing the work needed to push the gas out ("blowdown"); exhaust closes after TDC so the outgoing gas helps scavenge the cylinder.
  • Valve overlap: around TDC both valves are open together; the exhaust flow helps draw in fresh air.
  • Injection starts a little before TDC, because the fuel needs time (ignition delay) to self-ignite.

3. Fuel Supply and Forced Induction ★★★

3.1 The Carburettor

A carburettor is a device that blends air and fuel for an internal combustion engine. It works on Bernoulli's principle: the faster air moves, the lower its static pressure (and the higher its dynamic pressure). Air drawn in by the engine is accelerated through a narrow throat, the venturi; the low pressure there draws fuel from the float bowl through a calibrated jet into the airstream, where it atomises (Figure 3.1). The throttle (accelerator) linkage does not control the liquid fuel directly — it meters the air; the speed of the airflow, and therefore its pressure, determines how much fuel is drawn in. Low-speed and idling ports let the engine run with a nearly closed throttle before the main system works fully.

3.2 Fuel Injection and EFI

Fuel injection is a system for admitting fuel into an IC engine; it replaced carburettors in cars during the 1980s–1990s. Primary difference: fuel injection atomises the fuel by forcibly pumping it through a small nozzle under high pressure, whereas a carburettor relies on the suction created by intake air accelerated through a venturi to draw the fuel into the airstream.

  • Types of petrol injection: (1) port fuel injection (PFI) — one injector in each intake port; (2) throttle-body injection (TBI) — injector(s) above the throttle valve; also direct gasoline injection (DGI) into the cylinder.
  • Injectors: solenoid-operated (electronic) or mechanical.

Electronic fuel injection (EFI). Most systems are electronically controlled by an electronic control module (ECM / ECU), the "on-board computer". Various engine components and sensors send electric signals to the ECM, which continuously calculates how much fuel to inject and then opens the injectors for the right length of time, so the proper amount of fuel sprays out to give the desired air–fuel ratio (Figure 3.2). Working: an electric pump draws fuel from the tank through a filter and delivers it to the fuel rail; a pressure regulator keeps the rail pressure constant (≈ 3 bar), returning excess fuel to the tank. Each solenoid injector is a valve: when the ECM energises its coil, the needle lifts and fuel sprays into the port. Since the pressure is constant, the fuel quantity is proportional to the opening time (pulse width), which the ECM computes for every cycle from the sensor signals.

Advantages of petrol injection (lecture): (1) high power is developed; (2) quick starting and warm-up; (3) low specific fuel consumption. Also: accurate, equal mixture to every cylinder, lower emissions (works with the catalytic converter), no carburettor icing, better response.

3.3 Forced Induction: Supercharger and Turbocharger

In most engines atmospheric pressure is the only force pushing air into the intake manifold — they are naturally (normally) aspirated. The fuel that can be burnt is limited by the air that enters. An engine produces more power at the same speed if more air–fuel mixture is forced into the cylinder — forced induction, provided by a supercharger or a turbocharger: air pumps (blowers) that force more mixture into the engine (Figure 3.3).

4. Engine Cooling and Lubrication ★★★

4.1 Why an IC Engine Must Be Cooled

Combustion of fuel in the cylinders produces a considerable amount of heat — gas temperatures reach about 3316 °C. About 35 % of the heat is transferred to the cylinder walls, which constitutes a power loss but must be removed. If it is not:

  1. the lubricant might burn (oil film breaks down at high temperature) → scuffing and seizure of piston and cylinder;
  2. parts expand unevenly → loss of clearances, distortion of the head, cracked block;
  3. valves and pistons overheat → burnt valves, piston crown failure;
  4. petrol engines knock (detonate); volumetric efficiency falls.

So heat must be continuously removed while the engine runs. But the engine must not be over-cooled either: it should reach and hold its best operating temperature (≈ 85–95 °C) quickly, because a cold engine wastes fuel, wears faster and pollutes more. The cooling system controls engine temperature.

4.2 Methods of Cooling and the Water-Cooling System

  • Air cooling: the cylinder is cast with fins that increase the surface area; air flowing over the fins carries the heat away (motorcycles, scooters). Simple and light, but uneven cooling and noisy.
  • Water (liquid) cooling: water jackets are provided in the cylinder block and head; water fills them and the heat from the cylinder passes to the water, cooling the cylinder. The water is circulated by the thermosiphon effect (hot water rises, cold falls — no pump) or, in all modern cars, by a pump, with a fan and radiator.

The five basic parts (lecture):

  1. Water jackets surround the cylinders and combustion chambers; the coolant (water + antifreeze) flows through them, picking up heat — cooling the metal and heating the coolant.
  2. Water pump — engine-driven (belt) centrifugal pump that pushes the hot coolant out of the jackets and through the radiator.
  3. Thermostat — a thermostatic valve (wax pellet) between the engine and the radiator that controls coolant flow: closed when the engine is cold (coolant only circulates in the engine via the bypass → fast warm-up), opens at about 82–90 °C to send coolant to the radiator.
  4. Radiator — a heat exchanger with two sets of passages, one for the coolant (fine tubes) and one for the outside air (fins); coolant circulates continuously between the jackets and the radiator. The pressure cap (pressure valve, vacuum valve, seat, overflow pipe) pressurises the system (≈ 1 bar), raising the boiling point.
  5. Fan — pulls or pushes outside air through the radiator, improving cooling especially at idle and low speed (belt-driven or thermostatically controlled electric fan).

Circuit: pump → water jackets (block and head) → thermostat → top hose → radiator (cooled by air) → bottom hose → pump.

Advantages of water cooling over air cooling: uniform and efficient cooling of all cylinders; precise temperature control by the thermostat; quieter engine (the water jacket damps noise); compact engine with cylinders close together; heat available for the car's heater; suits high-output engines.

4.3 Antifreeze and Coolant

To prevent freezing of water in the cooling system, antifreeze is added to form the coolant — the liquid that circulates through the system, removes waste heat from the engine and delivers it to the radiator. The most common antifreeze is ethylene glycol (high-silicate or low-silicate types). A mixture of half water, half ethylene glycol is recommended for year-round use; it performs three jobs:

  1. Lowers the freezing point of the coolant to −37 °C (freezing water expands and would crack the block and radiator);
  2. Raises the boiling point to 108 °C, so the coolant is less likely to boil away in hot weather;
  3. Protects the cooling-system metals from deposits (scale) and corrosion (its inhibitors), and lubricates the water-pump seal.

4.4 Causes of Engine Overheating

  1. Low coolant level due to leakage of coolant.
  2. Accumulation of rust and scale, which prevents normal circulation of coolant.
  3. Collapsed hoses that prevent normal circulation.
  4. Thermostat stuck closed, blocking coolant circulation.
  5. Defective water pump that does not circulate enough coolant.
  6. A loose or worn drive belt that slips (pump and fan turn too slowly).
  7. After-boil and frozen coolant.

Other common causes: clogged radiator fins, faulty fan or fan switch, defective pressure cap, wrong ignition timing, lean mixture, overloading.

4.5 Lubrication System

Automobile lubrication is the supply of lubricating oil between the moving parts of the vehicle. Purposes of the lubricating oil (lecture):

  1. Lubricates moving parts to reduce wear (and friction, saving power).
  2. Cools — as it moves through the engine it picks up heat (e.g. from the pistons and bearings).
  3. Fills the clearances between bearings and rotating journals (hydrodynamic film, absorbs shock).
  4. Helps form a gas-tight seal between piston rings and cylinder walls.
  5. Acts as a cleaning agent — carries dirt and carbon to the filter.

Properties needed in engine oil: proper viscosity; high viscosity index (viscosity changes little with temperature); viscosity numbers (SAE grade, e.g. SAE 5W-20: "5W" = winter (cold) grade, "20" = viscosity at 100 °C); multi-viscosity oil; resistance to carbon formation and oil oxidation; corrosion and rust inhibitors; foaming resistance; detergent–dispersant additives; extreme-pressure resistance.

Parts that need lubrication: crankshaft main bearings, crankpins, big and small ends of the connecting rod, gudgeon-pin bushes, cylinder walls, piston rings, valve mechanism, timing gears, camshaft bearings.

Lubrication systems: (1) petroil (oil mixed with petrol — two-stroke engines); (2) splash (dippers on the connecting rods splash oil from the sump); (3) pressure-feed (pump forces oil through drilled passages to the bearings); (4) combined splash and pressure-feed (most car engines) — Figure 4.2.

Oil change: oil should be changed when dirty or contaminated — e.g. oil and filter every 5000 km (severe service) or every 12 500 km (normal), using the viscosity and service rating in the owner's manual. Common complaints: the engine uses too much oil; the oil-pressure light/gauge shows low pressure.

5. The Clutch ★★★

5.1 What the Clutch Does

The clutch is a device that couples and uncouples the manual transmission (or transaxle) and the engine. In vehicles with a manual transmission the power from the engine to the drive wheels flows through the clutch. It is usually operated by the driver's foot (some have a power-assist device): pushing the clutch pedal down disengages the clutch from the engine flywheel so no power flows; releasing the pedal engages it so power flows.

Why a clutch is needed: an IC engine cannot start under load and cannot run below about 600–800 rpm, while the car must start from rest; and gears cannot be changed while they are transmitting torque. The clutch therefore lets the engine start and idle without moving the car, lets the car start smoothly by gradually taking up the drive, and interrupts the drive for gear changes.

5.2 Functions of the Clutch

  1. It can be disengaged (pedal down): allows engine cranking and lets the engine run freely without delivering power to the transmission.
  2. While disengaged it permits the driver to shift the transmission into the required gear (first … fifth, reverse or neutral) for the operating condition.
  3. While engaging (pedal moving up) the clutch slips momentarily: this gives smooth engagement and lessens the shock on gears, shafts and other drive-train parts; as the engine develops enough torque to overcome the vehicle's inertia, the wheels turn and the vehicle moves.
  4. When engaged (pedal up) it transmits the engine power to the transmission with all slipping stopped.

5.3 Construction and Operation of a Single-Plate Clutch

The clutch consists of the engine flywheel, a friction disc and a pressure plate:

  • The flywheel and pressure plate are the driving members; they are attached to and rotate with the crankshaft.
  • The friction disc is the driven member: a steel disc with friction facings on both sides and torsional damper springs in the hub; its hub is splined to the transmission input shaft, so it turns the shaft but can slide back and forth on the splines.
  • The pressure plate, with one or more springs, is attached to the clutch cover; this pressure-plate assembly bolts to the flywheel and rotates with it. Spring force holds the friction disc against the flywheel while engaged.
  • The release (throw-out) bearing, clutch fork and linkage (rod, cable or hydraulic master/slave cylinders) connect the pedal to the pressure plate.

Operation. Engaged (pedal released): the springs clamp the friction disc between the pressure plate and the flywheel; friction makes the disc, and with it the transmission input shaft, turn with the flywheel. Engaging: as the pedal is released the springs gradually re-clamp the disc; it slips briefly, then locks up.

5.4 Coil-Spring and Diaphragm-Spring Clutches

In a coil-spring clutch several coil springs between the cover and the pressure plate provide the clamping force, and three release levers pull the plate back. In a diaphragm-spring clutch a single dished (Belleville) diaphragm spring made of spring steel, slotted into radial fingers, does both jobs. It is held by pivot rings on the cover:

  • Engaged: the dished spring presses on the pressure plate with its outer rim, clamping the friction disc against the flywheel.
  • Disengaged: the release bearing pushes the inner ends of the fingers toward the flywheel; the spring pivots about the rings like a lever (an "oil-can" action), so its outer rim moves away from the flywheel, pulling the pressure plate back (via retracting clips) and freeing the disc.

Advantages of the diaphragm spring: fewer parts, compact and lighter; uniform clamping all round; lower pedal effort (its force–deflection curve falls after a point); clamping force stays nearly constant as the facings wear (coil springs lose force as they extend); no release levers to adjust; better balance at high speed (no centrifugal effect on coil springs).

6. Manual Transmission and the Synchronizer ★★★

6.1 Purposes of the Transmission

The transmission (gearbox) — or transaxle when it is combined with the differential in a front-wheel-drive car — must (lecture):

  1. Provide the torque needed to move the vehicle under a variety of road and load conditions, by changing the gear ratio between the engine crankshaft and the drive wheels (high torque for starting and climbing, high speed for cruising);
  2. Be shifted into reverse so the vehicle can move backward (the engine turns only one way);
  3. Be shifted into neutral for starting the engine and running it without turning the drive wheels.

Why gear ratios are needed: an engine produces useful torque only in a limited speed band, while the tractive effort required varies enormously (starting on a hill vs cruising). The torque at the wheels is the engine torque multiplied by the gear ratio and the final-drive ratio:

6.2 Types of Transmission; Gears and Overdrive

There are two basic types: manual transmissions/transaxles, shifted by hand, and automatic ones, which shift with no help from the driver (Chapter 7). Older manual transmissions had 3 forward speeds (first/low, second, third/high) plus reverse and neutral; 4-speed units became common, and many are now 5-speed (or 6). Fourth gear in some 4-speed units and fifth in 5-speed units is overdrive: the output shaft turns faster than (overdrives) the input shaft, so a lower engine speed keeps the car at its road speed — better fuel economy, less engine wear, noise and vibration.

In a 3-speed box (lecture): in high gear the clutch and transmission shafts are locked together — 1 : 1, no speed reduction; overall ratios (with the final drive) are about 4.5–6 : 1 in second and 7.5–10 : 1 in low; reverse is about the same as low. With overdrive, the crankshaft speed is about 70 % of the propeller-shaft speed.

Gearbox types: sliding-mesh (gears slide on the main shaft into mesh — noisy, needs double-declutching), constant-mesh (all gears always in mesh; dog clutches lock the selected gear to the shaft) and synchromesh (constant mesh + synchronizers — all modern cars).

6.3 Components and Power Flow

Components of a gearbox: (1) gears that transmit the power; (2) splined shafts that rotate while other parts slide on them — the input (clutch) shaft, the countershaft (lay shaft) and the main (output) shaft; (3) bearings that support the shafts and transfer the load to the case; plus the reverse idler gear, synchronizers and the shift forks and linkage (Figure 6.1).

Power flow: the input-shaft gear drives the countershaft; the countershaft gears are in constant mesh with the main-shaft gears, which spin freely on the main shaft. Selecting a gear moves a synchronizer sleeve that locks that gear to the main shaft, so power flows input → countershaft → selected gear → sleeve and hub → main shaft. In top (direct) gear the sleeve locks the input shaft directly to the main shaft (1 : 1). In reverse, an idler gear is inserted between a countershaft gear and a main-shaft gear, reversing the direction of rotation.

6.4 The Synchronizer

Definition (lecture)
A synchronizer is used to slow down the faster-moving of two gears to be engaged until the two gears are revolving at the same speed, so that they can be engaged quickly, noiselessly and without clashing. Thus it helps in smooth engagement of gears. A transmission that uses synchronizers is a synchromesh transmission: the shifts are synchronized and the gears are in constant mesh.

Components (lecture):

  1. Synchronizing cones — a cone surface on the side of each gear, and a matching internal cone in the synchronizer (blocker) ring, which is a bronze/brass ring with external teeth.
  2. Synchronizer hub — splined to the transmission output shaft; it has three slots.
  3. Three keys (inserts) that fit into the slots in the hub.
  4. Two ring-shaped synchronizer springs that apply a slight outward force against the keys.
  5. Synchronizer sleeve — fits over the hub; its internal splines mesh with the external splines of the hub, and it can slide on them. It is moved by the shift fork. The keys have raised sections that fit in a detent groove inside the sleeve (a detent is a small depression into which another part moves to give a locking effect), so the sleeve carries the keys with it.

Synchronization is a three-stage action (lecture):

  1. Neutral → first contact. To shift into a gear the synchronizer sleeve is moved toward that gear. The sleeve slides on the hub splines and carries the three keys with it. The keys butt against the synchronizer ring and push it toward the gear, bringing the cone surface of the ring into contact with the cone surface on the gear.
  2. Synchronization. Friction between the synchronizer ring and the gear cone brings the two into synchronous rotation — they rotate at the same speed. Until they do, the ring's teeth are offset and block the sleeve (the "blocker" action), so a clash is impossible.
  3. Engagement (shift completed). When the external teeth on the ring and on the gear rotate at the same speed, the sleeve slides over them (the keys leave the detent). This locks the gear to the shaft and completes the shift. Power flows from the gear, through the synchronizer sleeve and hub, to the shaft. Similar actions take place for the other gears.

6.5 The Dog-and-Spline Clutch

A dog clutch is used to lock two shafts together or to lock a gear to a shaft. It consists of a sliding sleeve with two sets of internal splines: the smaller-diameter splines let it ride on the splined shaft and turn with it; the larger internal splines (teeth) are designed to match the external "dog" clutch teeth on the shaft or gear, and engage them when the sleeve is slid across. Since it can only be engaged when both sets of teeth match perfectly (same speed and alignment), dog clutches generally operate together with some form of synchronizing mechanism — the synchronizer sleeve of §6.4 is a dog clutch with synchronizing cones added.

6.6 Why Reverse Gear Often Has No Synchronizer

  1. Reverse is selected only when the vehicle is stationary: the output shaft is not turning, and with the clutch disengaged the input side quickly slows down, so there is (almost) no speed difference to synchronize.
  2. Reverse uses a sliding idler gear (often straight-cut spur gears) that is slid into mesh, not a constant-mesh gear locked by a sleeve, so there is no place for a synchronizer.
  3. Reverse is used rarely and for short periods; omitting a synchronizer saves cost, weight and length of the gearbox.
  4. A synchronizer must reverse the direction of a spinning part; trying to engage reverse while moving forward is undesirable anyway — the occasional grind warns the driver to stop first.

(The lecture notes that some transmissions do have a reverse synchronizer; most modern cars synchronize reverse for refinement.)

7.3 Automatic Transmission

An automatic transmission automatically provides several forward gear ratios between the engine crankshaft and the output shaft. It has three basic parts (lecture):

  1. A torque converter, usually with a lock-up clutch to prevent slippage — connects the crankshaft and transmits engine power to the gear train;
  2. A gear train, usually with one or more planetary gearsets (shifted by multiple-disc clutches and brake bands);
  3. A hydraulic system, which may be electronically controlled — hydraulic pressure acting through the automatic-transmission fluid (ATF) applies the clutches and bands and produces the shifts.

Operation: most automatics have 3–4 forward speeds (4th usually overdrive; some 5 or more) plus PARK, NEUTRAL and REVERSE. The car starts in first; the transmission upshifts into second, third and fourth, and the torque-converter clutch (TCC) locks, without driver assistance, as vehicle speed increases and throttle opening decreases. To stop, the driver releases the accelerator and brakes; the transmission unlocks the TCC and downshifts automatically to first. No foot-operated clutch is needed.

7.4 The Torque Converter

The torque converter is a form of fluid coupling — a device that uses a fluid (automatic-transmission fluid) and vaned rotors to transmit power between shafts. It is filled with ATF; when the engine runs, power flows from the crankshaft through the fluid to the transmission input shaft. A drive plate attaches to the crankshaft flange and the converter bolts to it. Inside the fluid-filled housing are three members (Figure 7.2):

  • Impeller (pump) — vaned rotor connected to the drive plate: the driving member, always at engine speed.
  • Turbine — vaned rotor connected to the transmission input shaft: the driven member, normally turning slower than the impeller.
  • Stator (reactor) — a stationary member with curved vanes placed between the turbine outlet and the impeller inlet, mounted on a one-way clutch.

How it works. When the engine runs, the fluid between the impeller vanes is thrown outward by centrifugal force. It strikes the turbine vanes, producing a rotating force on the turbine and the input shaft. The turbine vanes then direct the fluid back toward the centre of the impeller. The stator's curved vanes change the direction of the fluid leaving the turbine, so that it enters the impeller moving the same way the impeller turns, and then pushes on the turbine vanes again with a helping force. This continuing push increases the torque on the turbine — torque multiplication: in many converters the torque is more than doubled (1 lb-ft = 1.35 N·m in → more than 2 lb-ft = 2.7 N·m out). It occurs only when the impeller turns faster than the turbine — needed for acceleration from rest.

One-way clutch and coupling point. As the vehicle approaches cruising speed, the turbine "catches up" with the impeller; the fluid leaving the turbine moves at about impeller speed and starts to strike the backs of the stator vanes, so the stator would now be in the way. The one-way (roller or sprag) clutch locks the stator when the speed difference is large (fluid tries to spin it backwards) and lets it freewheel when the difference is small. From this coupling point (speed ratio ≈ 0.85–0.9) the converter acts as a simple fluid coupling (torque ratio 1). A lock-up clutch (TCC) then mechanically connects impeller and turbine, eliminating the remaining slip (≈ 2–5 %) to save fuel.

Example. Engine torque 200 N·m at stall (turbine held, car starting): torque ratio 2 → turbine torque =2×200==2\times200==2×200= 400 N·m into the gearbox — the converter acts like an extra, automatically varying gear.

ENERGY CHECKTorque doubles only when the turbine is much slower: output power = torque × speed is still less than the input; the difference is heat in the fluid.

Why a torque converter is used in an automatic transmission:

  1. It replaces the foot-operated clutch: it slips at idle, so the engine can idle in gear with the car stopped, and takes up the drive smoothly and automatically when the throttle is opened;
  2. It multiplies torque (up to ≈ 2–2.5 ×) for strong acceleration from rest and on hills;
  3. It damps engine torsional vibrations and drive-line shocks (fluid cushion) — smooth shifts, longer life;
  4. It provides a continuously variable torque ratio between stall and the coupling point, filling the gaps between gear steps.

8.2 Hydraulic Brakes

Most automotive service brakes are hydraulic: they operate by pressure applied through a liquid. They rely on Pascal's law — pressure applied to a confined liquid is transmitted equally in all directions — and on the fact that liquids are practically incompressible. The service brake system has two basic parts: the master cylinder and the wheel-brake mechanisms (drum and disc) (Figure 8.1).

Brake action (lecture): the master cylinder is a reciprocating-piston pump. When the driver depresses the brake pedal it pressurises the hydraulic system, converting the mechanical force of the pedal into hydraulic force. Brake fluid is forced from the master cylinder into the lines to the wheel brakes; as the pressure increases, brake shoes (drum) or pads (disc) are forced against the rotating drums or discs. The resulting friction slows or stops the wheels and the vehicle. When the pedal is released, return springs pull the shoes back and the fluid returns to the master cylinder.

Dual braking system: the master cylinder has two pistons and two pressure chambers; the front chamber feeds the front brakes and the rear chamber the rear brakes (or diagonal split). If one circuit leaks, the other still stops the car.

8.3 The Master Cylinder

Construction (lecture): rigidly fastened to the frame or the engine side of the firewall; a supply tank (reservoir) supplies fluid to the cylinder through two ports: the larger inlet (intake) port connects to the hollow portion behind the piston head; the smaller relief (compensating / bypass) port connects the reservoir directly with the cylinder and lines when the piston is in the released position. Inside: piston(s) with primary and secondary rubber cups, return spring, check valve, outlets to the lines.

Working:

  1. Released (pedal off): the piston rests back; the relief port is open, so fluid can flow between the reservoir and the cylinder — expansion of the fluid with heat, or wear of the linings, is compensated.
  2. Applying: the pedal pushes the piston forward; its primary cup covers the relief port, sealing the chamber; further movement forces fluid under pressure into the lines to the wheel cylinders/calipers.
  3. Releasing: the return spring quickly forces the piston back. Fluid from the reservoir flows through the inlet port and around the cup to fill the space in front of the piston (preventing a vacuum); when the piston is fully back, the relief port reopens and returning fluid from the lines flows back to the reservoir.
  4. Dual (tandem) type: the primary piston pressurises the first chamber, whose pressure pushes the secondary piston to pressurise the second chamber — two independent circuits.

8.4 Shoes, Linings, Brake Fluid, Power Brakes and ABS

  • Brake shoes and linings: shoes are metal; a facing of friction material — the lining — is riveted or bonded to them. Linings are non-asbestos (fibreglass or semi-metallic) materials that withstand the heat of braking; asbestos is being phased out because of its danger to health.
  • Brake fluid: a hydraulic fluid that transfers force into pressure and amplifies the braking force (liquids are not appreciably compressible); chemically inert; transmits force and motion and lubricates the pistons in master cylinder, wheel cylinders and calipers. Classified by the Department of Transportation as DOT 3, DOT 4 and DOT 5 (printed on the container).
  • Power brakes: use engine vacuum and/or battery power to enhance braking, so only a light pedal force is needed: vacuum-suspended, air-suspended, hydraulic booster, electro-hydraulic booster.
  • ABS: tyres skid when they decelerate faster than the vehicle. During hard braking ABS prevents wheel lock-up: it lets the brakes apply until the tyres are about to skid, then rapidly modulates the hydraulic pressure at each wheel ("pumping the brakes"), keeping the wheels turning so the car stays steerable (studied in Part B).

9. Suspension System ★★★

9.1 Purpose of the Suspension System

The suspension system is located between the wheel axles and the vehicle body or frame. Its purpose is to (lecture):

  1. Support the weight of the vehicle;
  2. Cushion bumps and holes in the road;
  3. Maintain traction between the tyres and the road;
  4. Hold the wheels in alignment.

It lets the vehicle travel over rough surfaces with a minimum of up-and-down body movement, and corner with minimum roll or tendency to lose traction. The cushioning action means road shocks — the actions resulting from the tyres moving up and down as they meet bumps or holes — have a minimal effect on the occupants and the load.

9.2 Springs and Shock Absorbers

The springs and the shock absorbers are the two main parts. The springs support the weight of the vehicle and its load and absorb road shocks (by deflecting and storing energy). The shock absorbers control (dampen) the spring action: without them, spring oscillation occurs — the springs keep the wheels and body bouncing up and down after they pass bumps or holes. Shock absorbers allow the basic spring movement but quickly dampen out the unwanted bouncing that follows.

Types of springs:

  • Coil spring — round spring-steel rod wound into a coil; a tapered rod gives a variable rate (stiffer as it compresses).
  • Leaf spring — layers of curved steel leaves clamped together; also locates the axle (trucks, rigid rear axles).
  • Torsion bar — straight spring-steel rod fixed to the frame at one end and to a control arm at the other; as the arm swings, the bar twists.
  • Air spring — rubber air bag filled with compressed air; a levelling valve and compressor add or release air as the load changes (constant ride height).

9.3 The Shock Absorber

A shock absorber is a tubular hydraulic device placed near each wheel; one end attaches to the vehicle body or frame, the other to a moving suspension part such as the axle housing or a control arm. Movement of the spring makes the shock absorber lengthen or shorten.

Purpose: its main purpose is to dampen the oscillations caused by the springs. It does not support the weight of the vehicle, and does not affect the vehicle height.

Operation: it is basically an oil-filled cylinder in which a piston moves up and down. The piston divides the cylinder into upper and lower chambers; its orifices (small holes) are restricted by spring-loaded check valves that deflect under pressure. As the suspension moves, the piston forces oil through the small passages; the resulting fluid friction resists the motion and converts the spring's energy into heat, limiting the spring's bounce. Different valves for compression (bump) and extension (rebound) give different damping in each direction (usually stiffer on rebound). The more easily the fluid flows, the softer and smoother the ride. A twin-tube design has a reservoir between inner and outer tubes for the oil displaced by the rod; a gas-charged type uses pressurised nitrogen to prevent foaming.

9.4 Dependent and Independent Suspension

In a conventional (dependent, rigid-axle) suspension, the two wheels are mounted on one solid axle, usually on leaf springs: a bump under one wheel tilts the whole axle and disturbs the other wheel. In independent suspension, each wheel is mounted separately (control arms or a strut), so each wheel moves up and down without affecting the other. The lecture lists coil springs, torsion bars and pneumatic (air) springs for independent suspension. Common types: MacPherson strut (strut = spring + damper acts as the upper pivot; compact, most front axles), double wishbone (two A-arms; precise wheel control), trailing-arm and multi-link.

Advantages of independent suspension: better ride comfort (lower unsprung mass, one wheel's bump not transmitted to the other); better road holding and steering (tyres stay in contact, less wheel shimmy and tramp); less body roll and better cornering; more room for the engine (lower floor, no axle beam). Disadvantages: more parts and joints, higher cost, more alignment maintenance; changes of wheel camber with movement.

10. Steering System ★★★

10.1 The Steering System

Definition (lecture)
The steering system allows the driver to control the direction of vehicle travel. This is made possible by linkage that connects the steering wheel to the steerable wheels and tyres.

Types: when the only energy source is the force the driver applies to the steering wheel, the vehicle has manual steering. Power steering uses a hydraulic pump or electric motor to assist the driver's effort; most vehicles have it, to make parking easier.

Components (lecture): (1) the steering wheel and steering shaft, which transmit the driver's movement to the steering gear; (2) the steering gear, which increases the mechanical advantage while changing the rotary motion of the steering wheel into linear motion; (3) the steering linkage (tie rods), which carries the linear motion to the steering arms on the steering knuckles. Steering gear types: recirculating-ball, rack-and-pinion (most cars), worm-and-roller.

10.2 Working Principle

The basic operation is the same for manual and power steering (lecture): as the driver turns the steering wheel, the movement is carried by the shaft to the steering gear, which changes the rotary motion into linear motion. The linear motion acts through the steering linkage or tie rods, attached to the steering-knuckle arms. The steering knuckles then pivot inward or outward on ball joints, moving the wheels and tyres left or right. In rack-and-pinion steering, the pinion on the shaft drives a toothed rack sideways; the tie rods at the rack ends push and pull the steering arms. The linkage geometry (Ackermann) makes the inner wheel turn more than the outer one in a corner.

10.3 Steering Ratio

The driver applies a relatively small force at the steering wheel, which results in a much larger steering force at the wheels — due to the mechanical advantage, or steering ratio, of the steering gear.

Definition
Steering ratio is the number of degrees that the steering wheel must turn to pivot the front wheels one degree. If the wheel must turn 17.5° to pivot the front wheels 1°, the steering ratio is 17.5 : 1.

Typical values: about 24 : 1 with manual steering to 14 : 1 with power steering; heavy vehicles 21 : 1 to 24 : 1; racing cars close to 1 : 1. The higher the ratio, the easier it is to steer (less effort) but the more the wheel must be turned. No road car uses 1 : 1: the effort would be too large and the steering so sensitive that a small jerk on the wheel would turn the vehicle equally — uncomfortable and dangerous. As vehicle weight increases, the force required increases, so heavy vehicles use larger ratios; power steering allows a lower (quicker) ratio because the booster supplies the effort.

10.4 Power Steering

A vehicle with power steering has an energy source that aids the driver in turning the wheels. Most systems are hydraulic: a pump supplies high-pressure fluid when the driver turns the wheel, providing most of the steering effort. Some cars use electric power steering (EPS), with an electric motor. Most power steering systems are basically a manual system with a power booster added — a power cylinder and piston built into the steering gear (integral power steering) or connected between the frame and the linkage (linkage type).

Working principle (lecture): all hydraulic power-steering systems work the same way. The hydraulic pump pressurises the fluid. Steering effort applied to the steering wheel causes the control valve to open and close fluid passages; these either admit pressurised fluid into the power cylinder (on one side of the piston) or relieve the pressure. The pressurised fluid causes the piston to move, providing most of the steering effort. In practice the valve is a rotary valve on a torsion bar: driver torque twists the bar slightly, opening the valve in proportion; when the driver stops turning, the bar re-centres the valve and the assist stops. If the pump fails, the car can still be steered manually (with more effort).

Components: power-steering pump (belt-driven, with reservoir and pressure-relief valve), hoses and fittings, power-steering fluid, power-steering pressure switch, fluid cooler, control valve, power cylinder.

Advantages over normal (manual) steering:

  1. Much less driver effort, especially when parking and at low speed, and with heavy vehicles or wide tyres;
  2. Allows a lower (quicker) steering ratio (≈ 14 : 1 vs 24 : 1) — fewer turns lock to lock, faster response;
  3. Less fatigue and better control on long drives;
  4. Absorbs road shocks and kickback (the fluid cushions the wheel), better stability after a tyre blow-out;
  5. Electric versions allow speed-sensitive assist and driver-assistance functions (lane keeping, parking assist).

11. Tyres ★★★

11.1 Functions and Types of Tyres

A tyre is a ring-shaped component that surrounds a wheel's rim to transfer the vehicle's load from the axle through the wheel to the ground and to provide traction on the road surface. Tyres are cushions that absorb most of the shocks caused by road irregularities: they flex as they meet the irregularities, reducing the effect of road shocks on the vehicle, passengers and load. They grip the road for good traction, so the vehicle can accelerate, brake and turn without skidding.

Tube and tubeless tyres: a tube tyre has an inner tube (a round rubber container holding the air) with a valve through the rim — used in some truck and motorcycle tyres. Tubeless tyres, used by most cars, mount on an airtight rim; air is held between the rim flange and the tyre bead. (Tubeless: lose air slowly when punctured, run cooler, easier to repair.)

11.2 Bias-Ply and Radial Tyres

The tyre carcass is made of plies (layers of rubber-coated cords), applied in two ways:

  • Bias (diagonal) ply: plies run diagonally, crisscrossing each other (≈ 30–40° to the centre line). The tyre is strong in all directions because the plies overlap, but the plies tend to move against each other and produce heat, especially at high speed, and the tread tends to close up or "squirm" as it meets the road.
  • Radial ply: plies run parallel to each other and at 90° (vertical) to the tyre bead; stabiliser belts (steel or fabric) are laid over the plies under the tread to give strength parallel to the beads. All new cars and most light-duty vehicles have radial tyres. The radial sidewall is more flexible, and the tread wraps round the edge to compensate; so the tread does not heel up as much in a curve — more tread stays on the road and the tendency to skid is reduced.

Rotation: front and rear tyres do different jobs (steering, driving), and vehicle type and driving habits make them wear differently. Causes of excessive tyre wear (lecture): high speed and improper tyre pressure (premature failure and excessive wear); frequent starts and stops, severe braking and acceleration (rapid wear); misaligned and unbalanced wheels (uneven wear). Merits of radial over bias ply: (1) lower rolling resistance → better fuel economy; (2) longer tread life — the belted tread does not squirm; (3) less heat build-up — better at high speed; (4) better cornering and skid resistance — the flexible sidewall keeps the full tread on the road; (5) better braking and wet grip, more comfortable at highway speed.

Demerits: (1) harsher ride at low speed over small irregularities; (2) higher cost; (3) flexible sidewall more easily damaged (kerbs) and harder to judge pressure visually; (4) should not be mixed with bias tyres on the same axle; requires correct pressure for its benefits.
